# Visual design + teach-back: evidence for our concept pages

## 1. Visual design that sticks
- Mayer's principles, median d from his own reviews: coherence (cut extras) ~0.86, spatial contiguity (labels on the diagram) ~0.8-1.1, segmenting (learner-paced chunks) ~0.7, signaling (highlight key path) 0.4-0.86. Redundancy is unstable (0.10-0.87). Effects are strongest for low-knowledge learners. [UNH summary](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf), [Chartered College](https://my.chartered.college/impact_article/how-multimedia-can-improve-learning-and-instruction/)
- Tired learner: a night of sleep loss before learning hurts later memory (g = 0.62), so cutting load matters more. Coherence, contiguity and segmenting come first. [Newbury 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8893218/)
- Worked examples help novices most and lose value as expertise grows (expertise reversal). [Kalyuga 2007](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-007-9054-3)
- Concreteness fading moves from a concrete case to an abstract form. It is supported by a systematic review, mostly in math and science (weak evidence for software topics). [Fyfe et al. 2014, cited in](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.14562)

## 2. Diagram types and fill-in diagrams
- Matching the diagram to the concept (process → flow or cycle, comparison → table, scope → nested boxes, decision → tree) is design convention. I found no head-to-head trials (weak evidence).
- Filling in a partly blank concept map from memory beats rereading. [Learning Scientists](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/4/6-1), [Karpicke & Blunt 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21252317/)
- It does not clearly beat other active methods once study time is equal. [2024 preregistered replication](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/pmid/38213612)

## 3. Learning by teaching
- Self-explanation prompts: g = 0.55 across 64 reports. Question-style prompts work better than commands (0.56 vs 0.40). Multiple-choice prompts are weaker (weak evidence). [Bisra et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x)
- Teaching: g = 0.27 overall, about 0.48 when learners study expecting to teach. [Kobayashi 2024](https://x-mol.com/paper/1763030854519197696)
- The protégé effect comes from teachable-agent work (Chase et al. 2009). The Feynman technique is untested as a named method (weak evidence). [EdSurge](https://edsurge.com/news/2019-01-24-want-students-to-remember-what-they-learn-have-them-teach-it)
- A good prompt is open-ended and asks why. It names the audience and says in advance that the learner will explain.

## 4. Transfer
- Comparing two cases side by side transfers much better than studying them one at a time (analogical encoding). [Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson 2003](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2003/learning-and-transfer-a-general-role-for-analogical-encoding/)
- Explaining two analogous cases raises spontaneous transfer (Edwards et al. 2014, same source).
- Analyzing contrasting cases before being told the rule prepares learners to learn from the explanation ("time for telling"). [Schwartz & Bransford 1998](https://www.wright.edu/sites/www.wright.edu/files/uploads/2017/Feb/event/Schwartz_Bransford_1998_TimeForTelling.pdf), [Schwartz et al. 2011](https://ece.northeastern.edu/edsnu/mcgruer/USC/PracticingVersusInventing2011-edu-103-4-759.pdf)

## 5. Feedback on explanations
- Feedback overall: d = 0.48, with big differences between studies. The more information it carries, the more it helps, and it works best on cognitive outcomes. [Wisniewski et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456)
- Elaborated feedback (explains why) beats right/wrong feedback (ES about 0.49, Van der Kleij et al. 2015, secondary source). [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.645758/pdf)
- Immediate feedback is usually recommended for hard tasks and struggling learners (Shute 2008; not verified this session, weak evidence).

## Design rules for our concept pages
1. One diagram per page with no decoration. Labels sit on the parts they name (coherence, contiguity: §1).
2. Highlight the single key path or failure point (signaling: §1).
3. Reveal the diagram one step at a time with a "next" button (segmenting: §1).
4. Open with one concrete scenario, then show the abstract label (concreteness fading: §1, weak evidence).
5. Choose the diagram by concept type: loop → cycle, scope → nested boxes, choice → decision tree, comparison → two-column table (§2, weak evidence).
6. Put a correct and a broken example side by side. Ask "what differs?" before explaining (§4).
7. After study, show the same diagram with 2-3 blanks to fill from memory (§2).
8. Announce the teach-back up front. Use a free-text "why" prompt aimed at a named novice, never multiple choice (§3).
9. Rotate three prompts: why the wrong option fails, apply it to a new scenario, and the shared rule in two cases (§3, §4).
10. Grade right away against three points (mechanism, causal why, misconception). Quote what is missing, give one fix, then allow a retry (§5).
